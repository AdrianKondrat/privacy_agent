# Koverr lead-magnet research dossier

Method: PhD Research Team pipeline (stages 0–5). The user asked for research then build in one go, so the two gates (after Stage 0 and Stage 2) were run through rather than paused; Frame and Map are shown in full below.

## Stage 0: Research Frame

**Topic and decision.** Which 3–4 PDF freebies should Koverr build first so that worried, non-expert adults trade their email for one and join the launch list, and what must each contain to be accurate and on-brand?

**Context (from PRODUCT.md, docs/01-strategy.md).** Founder: solo, pre-launch. Horizon: build in days, use for the next 3–6 months of waitlist growth. Geography: UK/EU first, US second; the launch list's region answer decides. Constraints: no budget for paid design; product claims must never imply guaranteed removal, whole-web coverage, or "you are safe"; legal templates are not legal advice. Brand: exposure in greyscale, colour (one green) only for fixes. Signups come from trigger moments: breach email, scam surge, finding own address online, fraud scare, apps sharing data.

**Definitions.**
- *Lead magnet / freebie:* a free asset given in exchange for an email address.
- *Opt-in rate:* signups ÷ visitors who see the offer.
- *Activation:* the share of downloaders who complete at least one action from the PDF (proxy for trust in the product).
- *Leak path:* one of Koverr's six routes — stolen, sold, shared, published, posted, indexed.
- *Actionable:* a step the reader can finish in under 15 minutes with a link or exact wording.

**Sub-questions.**
1. Which lead-magnet formats convert best for consumer (not B2B) audiences, and does a "specific, fast, personal" freebie beat a long guide?
2. Which formats map to Koverr's trigger moments and preview the product's value (find → fix → proof) without giving the product away?
3. Which formats spread on their own (shared, forwarded, posted) and which help the founder learn about the list (region, worry)?
4. What facts must be current and correct in November 2026 (laws, opt-out services, breach-checking tools), and which are risky to print?
5. What can one person produce well in days?

**Expert lineup.** Default five: practitioner (a growth marketer who has run consumer waitlists), skeptic, economist, historian, academic. Affected party not otherwise represented: the worried reader themself — represented through the practitioner's and academic's focus on behaviour, flagged here.

**Candidate freebies to evaluate (long list).**
A. Breach-email decoder: what each leaked data type means and the exact fix.
B. 30-minute privacy lockdown checklist across six paths.
C. AI prompt pack: prompts to audit your own footprint, read privacy policies, draft requests.
D. "Who has my data?" request letter kit (GDPR Art. 15/17/21, CCPA) with fill-in templates.
E. Self-scored exposure report: a worksheet that scores your exposure and outputs a ranked fix list.
F. Scam-call tracer: how they got your number and how to cut it off.
G. Home-address removal guide (people-search, electoral register, Google "Results about you").
H. Annual privacy-check calendar / 12-month plan.

**Claims to test (skeptic targets).**
1. "Checklists and templates are the highest-converting lead-magnet formats."
2. "Fear-based framing ('your data is leaking right now') drives more signups than help-based framing."
3. "A freebie that solves the problem fully reduces demand for the paid product."
4. "AI prompt packs are a strong lead magnet in 2026."

## Stage 1: Independent perspectives

Method: five isolated subagents, each given only the Research Frame, each with web search. Order of return recorded as received.

### Skeptic (SK) — 10 searches

**Position.** The default playbook (fear hook → checklist → templates → AI prompt pack) maximises signups but attracts freebie-hunters, creates liability and goes stale, which is the worst trade for a trust brand. Kill C, D (as a standalone magnet), H and the "score" part of E. B survives only cut to 5–7 steps. A, F and G survive best: trigger-tied, narrow, preview find → fix → proof without promising safety.

| ID | Claim | Source / label | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| SK1 | No solid evidence that checklists/templates convert best; format numbers are vendor figures on different traffic; traffic source swings rates as much as format | cited: getresponse.com/blog/best-lead-magnets-study; shno.co lead-magnet stats; digitalapplied.com 2026 benchmarks | med-high |
| SK2 | Efficacy framing beats fear framing for getting people to act; fear may still win the click | cited: Lowry et al. 2023 JMIS meta-analysis (jmis-web.org/articles/1641); Wall & Buche; NIST security fatigue (scmagazine.com) | med-high on activation, med on opt-in |
| SK3 | "Complete freebie cannibalises paid" is mostly backwards for Koverr: paid value is ongoing, fixes decay (brokers relist). Mismatched freebies are the bigger risk | cited: Optery help article; diymarketers.com; emailtooltester.com | med |
| SK4 | AI prompt packs are commodity and off-brand: they teach people to paste personal data into chatbots. ~30% of UK adults have shared confidential data with chatbots; 53% of US adults did not know chatbots train on chats by default | cited: lilachbullock.com; techradar.com (NymVPN survey); 9to5mac.com 2026-08-25 (DuckDuckGo survey) | med-high on trust risk |
| SK5 | A standalone GDPR letter kit is the highest-liability, least differentiated item: ICO/Which?/Liberty give templates free; UK DUAA 2025 changed SARs ("reasonable and proportionate" search; clock can pause for clarification; in force 19 June 2025); ICO guidance under review | cited: footanstey.com DUAA article; ICO templates PDF; which.co.uk SAR | med-high |

| SK7 | A self-score risks false reassurance and breaks "never say you are safe" | inferred | med |
| SK8 | A 12-month calendar answers no trigger moment; lowest opt-in | inferred | med |
| SK9 | Optimise activation-weighted opt-in, not raw opt-in: a scared, inactive subscriber is reputational debt | inferred from SK2 | med |

**Blind spot.** Evidence is about behaviour change, not email opt-in. No controlled consumer study of lead-magnet formats exists, so claim 1 is unproven rather than false.

### Academic (AC) — 12 searches

**Position.** Pair a clear threat with a short, ranked list of steps. Fear without efficacy is weak or backfires; people don't lack advice, they lack an order. People barely act after breach notices, and many feel resigned. Top 4: A, E, G, B.

| ID | Claim | Source / label | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| AC1 | Fear helps only with response- and self-efficacy information; fear level alone did not predict persuasion | cited: Bigsby & Albarracín 2021 (asc.upenn.edu PDF); Boise State meta-analysis | high on efficacy; med on opt-in |
| AC2 | The problem is prioritisation: 374 advice items across 1,264 documents; experts named 118 different items in their top five ("crisis of prioritization") | cited: Redmiles et al., USENIX Security 2020 | high |
| AC3 | Expert vs non-expert gap: experts put updates, 2FA and password managers first; non-experts favour antivirus and "only visit known sites". Gap persisted in 2019 replication | cited: Ion, Reeder & Consolvo SOUPS 2015; Busse et al. SOUPS 2019 | high on gap |
| AC4 | After a breach only 33% of affected users changed the password, 13% within 3 months; new passwords often similar or weaker | cited: Bhagavatula et al. 2020, arxiv.org/abs/2010.09853 | med-high (small N) |
| AC5 | Digital resignation is common; fatalism makes protection feel futile; quick visible wins counter it (inferred) | cited: Draper & Turow 2019; VU special issue 2024 | med |
| AC6 | Manual people-search opt-outs removed ~70% of listings in a week vs 0–58.7% for paid services (small US sample, not significant) | cited: Consumer Reports / Tall Poppy, Aug 2024 | med |
| AC7 | Personalised, timely feedback changes security behaviour more than generic messages; no RCT on self-scored privacy worksheets | cited: Bender et al. 2024; Peer et al. CHB 2020 | med |
| AC8 | Checklist-converts-best not verified | recalled-unverified | low-med |
| AC9 | Optimise activation: design each PDF around one 5-minute "first win" plus a proof step | inferred | med |

**Citable stats for PDFs:** FTC 2025 data — $15.9bn reported fraud losses, ~1.4m identity-theft reports (confirm FTC URL); Bhagavatula 33%; Consumer Reports 70% manual opt-out. Do not print a hard HIBP total; do not print "74% resigned".

**Blind spot.** Evidence is about acting, not about handing over an email; mostly US samples.

## Stage 3: Research briefing

**1. CEO summary.** For a solo, pre-launch, UK-first privacy brand, build four short PDFs, each tied to a trigger moment the landing pages already target, each opening with a 5-minute first win and a ranked "do these first" list, and each pointing only at free official tools. The order of value is a breach-email decoder (/breach/), a home-address guide (/home-address/), a scam-call tracer with one access-request letter (/scam-calls/), and a self-scored exposure snapshot for the homepage and leak map (/, /leak-map/). Skip a standalone AI prompt pack; use prompts only where they need no private data. Every PDF should date itself so it expires, which is the reason to stay on the list for Koverr's watch plan.

**2. Key findings (ranked by Stage 2 score)**
1. **Rank, don't list.** People fail at prioritisation, not information; every PDF leads with three steps and the time each takes. (AC2, PR6, SK fatigue; score 10)
2. **Point to official and first-party tools; never promise removal.** Removal services vanish and underperform; DROP, council opt-outs, TPS, credit freezes, Google's tool are durable. (PR4, HI7, AC6, SK6; 10)
3. **Fear must arrive with the fix.** Name the trigger in the title; every alarming fact sits next to a step. (AC1, SK2, HI6; 9 — dissent: may lower raw opt-in, unmeasured)
4. **Breach notices leave people stuck; the decoder fills that gap.** (AC4, PR3, HI2; 8)
5. **A self-assessment with a personal, dated output beats a static guide on both opt-in and data, provided it gives fixes, not a safety verdict.** (EC4, PR2, AC7 vs SK7; 5 — weakest evidence, vendor-heavy)

**3. Hidden connection.** The panel's reasons to *fear* each freebie are the same as the reasons Koverr's paid product exists: templates decay with the law (HI-X), listings relist (SK3, EC6), scores go stale (SK7, EC9), removal vendors vanish (PR4, HI7). A freebie that honestly *dates itself* — "checked 12 Nov 2026", "your snapshot on …", "recheck in 90 days" — turns each weakness into the reason to stay subscribed, without fear copy. No single expert saw this: the historian saw decay as a precedent, the economist as a pricing lever, the skeptic as a liability. (HI-X, EC9, SK3, SK7, PR4)

**4. One action.** Ship A, G, F (+ letter) and E, each mapped 1:1 to an existing landing page, with a dated "last checked" stamp and a "recheck" date that the launch emails honour.
(a) Test: send each campaign page's traffic its own PDF for two weeks, then a day-3 "did you do step 1?" email; record signup rate and reply rate per page. (b) Wrong if a page's signups with the PDF are no higher than without, or day-3 replies show under 1 in 10 did step 1. (c) Rests on AC2, AC1, AC4, PR5, PR9, HI-X, EC9.

**5. Open questions.** Does the PDF raise opt-in for UK readers specifically? Does a web scorer beat the printable E? Does fear vs trigger framing change activation? Is the letter in F used, and does it get answers? Settled by the per-page test above and by replies.

## Stage 4: Independent audit (fresh subagent, dossier only)

**Verdict: verify first.** The four wrong print facts below are fixed, Google's UK/EU availability is hedged, and the findings are downgraded.

| Finding | Stage 2 | Audit | Why lower |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Rank, don't list | 10 | 6 | AC2 shows experts disagree on priorities; it does not show readers act more on a ranked PDF |
| 2 Official tools; never promise removal | 10 | 6 | One shutdown generalised; first-party tools changed too (HIBP, Meta) |
| 3 Fear with the fix | 9 | 7 | Two meta-analyses support efficacy for action; opt-in effect unmeasured |
| 4 Decoder fills post-breach gap | 8 | 4 | One small study; "fills the gap" is inference |
| 5 Self-assessment beats static | 5 | 3 | Vendor quiz benchmarks only; no trial |

**Traceability problems.**
- The hidden connection restates HI-X and EC9 rather than synthesising new ground. It is now treated as a hypothesis credited to them, to be tested.
- The action's thresholds have no source.
- Picking F was weakly supported. It was kept because it maps to the existing /scam-calls/ page and carries the historian's letter (D) inside it.

**Weakest claim.** That a dated, expiring freebie keeps subscribers and drives conversion (HI-X, EC9, both inferred). If it is wrong, the four PDFs still stand as useful content; only the "recheck" hook weakens.

**Bias.**
- Unused claims: SK1, SK9, HI1, HI3, HI5, AC3, AC5, EC3 and EC8.
- Evidence is almost entirely US for a UK-first product.
- Stage 2 gaps were dropped from the briefing; the stalking signpost is restored in the address guide.
