Step 1 · Trace it

Your number got out one of four ways.

The clue is usually in what the caller or text already knows. Match it to a row.

PathHow it happensThe clueHow to check
Stolen
Scam texts		 A company you used was breached and your number was in the data. It is now on lists that scammers trade. Texts naming a real company you use: a delivery firm, your bank, a shop. Search your email at haveibeenpwned.com and read which breaches included phone numbers.
Sold
Sales calls		 A data broker or list seller bought it from a form, competition, survey or app and resold it. Sales calls about things you never asked for: energy, insurance, “an accident you had”. Ask the caller which company they are and where they got your number. Legitimate marketers must say. Then send that company the letter on page 5.
Shared
Hidden		 An app, shop or service passed it to “partners” under a privacy policy you clicked through. Calls or texts soon after signing up for something. Think back to what you signed up for just before it started. Send that company the letter on page 5 and ask who received your number.
Posted or public
Easy to fix		 On an old profile, a business listing, a classified ad, a CV site, or a people-search page. Callers who know your name and town but nothing else. Search your number in quotes in a search engine and in the social apps you use.

A trick for next time

For new sign-ups, use an email alias or a variation such as yourname+shopname@ (many email services deliver these to your normal inbox). If a scam arrives addressed to that variation, you know which company it came from. It is a strong hint, not proof.

Step 2 · Cut it off · 15 minutes

Cut the calls in the order that works.

Preference lists stop companies that follow the law. Phone settings stop everyone else. Do both.

Turn on spam filtering on your phone

iPhone: Settings → Apps → Phone → Silence Unknown Callers (on older versions, Settings → Phone). Unknown numbers go to voicemail. Android: in the Phone app, Settings → Caller ID & spam, then turn on spam filtering. Wording varies by phone maker.

2 min

Add a PIN for SIM swaps and number transfers

Call your mobile network or use its app and ask for a PIN or password on any SIM change or number transfer. This stops the most damaging use of a leaked number.

5 min

Register on the official do-not-call list UK US

UK: Telephone Preference Service, tpsonline.org.uk, free. US: National Do Not Call Registry, donotcall.gov, free. It can take up to a month to take full effect. It stops legitimate sales calls, not scammers or overseas callers. The real services never charge.

3 min

Report scam texts by forwarding them

UK: forward the text to 7726, free on all major networks; forward scam emails to report@phishing.gov.uk. US: forward to 7726 (SPAM), then report at reportfraud.ftc.gov.

1 min

Hide your number where you posted it

Facebook, Instagram, LinkedIn and others let people find you by your number. Turn off “find me by phone number” and hide it from your profile.

5 min

On the call itself

Don’t

Confirm your name, date of birth or address. Read out a code you were texted. Move money to a “safe account”. Install an app a caller asks for.

Do

Hang up and call back on the number on your card or the company’s website. In the UK, call 159 to reach most major banks safely. Ask a sales caller: “Which company are you, and where did you get my number?”

Lost money or gave details?

Call your bank straight away on the number on your card (UK: 159). Then report it. UK: Report Fraud, which replaced Action Fraud, at reportfraud.police.uk or 0300 123 2040 (Scotland: Police Scotland on 101). US: reportfraud.ftc.gov, and identitytheft.gov for identity theft.

Got the company’s name?

Send it the letter on the next page. You can ask any organisation that holds your data where it came from and who it went to. That turns “how did they get my number?” into a written answer.

Step 4 · Make them tell you

The “where did you get my number?” letter.

UK and EU law lets you ask any organisation for a copy of your data, where it came from and who received it. Californians have a similar right to know. Email it to the company’s privacy or data protection contact, from your own email.

Subject: Subject access request: source and recipients of my data

Hello,

I am making a subject access request under Article 15 of the [UK GDPR / GDPR]. You contacted me on [number] on [date]. To find my record, please use: [name, phone number, email].

Please tell me:

1. What personal data you hold about me.
2. Where you obtained it, including the name of any company you got it from.
3. Who you have shared or sold it to, by name where possible.
4. Why you are using it, and how long you will keep it.

I also object to the use of my data for direct marketing under Article 21, and ask you to stop calling and texting me.

I understand you normally have one month to respond, and that a reasonable and proportionate search is expected. If you need more information to find my data, please ask for only what is necessary.

Thank you,
[name] · [date]

California: replace the first paragraph with a request to know under the California Consumer Privacy Act, including “the categories of sources and third parties”; businesses normally have 45 days. Not legal advice. The UK changed its rules in 2025: searches must be “reasonable and proportionate”, and the deadline can pause while a company asks you to clarify. For official guidance and templates, see ico.org.uk. If you get no answer after the deadline, complain to the company, then to the regulator.
