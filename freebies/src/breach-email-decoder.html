Step 1 · Read it properly

Get four answers out of the email in a minute.

Breach notices are written by lawyers to be accurate, not useful. Pull these four facts out and the rest of this guide tells you what to do with them.

1 · Who was breached?

The company that lost the data. Not always the one you think: it may be a supplier, a loyalty scheme or a service you signed up to years ago.

2 · What was taken?

Look for a list such as “names, email addresses, dates of birth”. These are the rows you look up on pages 3 and 4. If the email does not say, assume email address and password.

3 · Was your password in it?

“Hashed” or “encrypted” passwords are harder to use, not impossible. Treat any password in a breach as known.

4 · Is the email real?

Scammers send fake breach notices. Do not click the links. Open the company’s website or app yourself and look for the notice there.

Step 2 · Decode it

What leaked, what it means, what fixes it.

Find each detail from the email. The shade shows how much harm it can do; the green column is the step that makes it useless.

What leakedWhat someone can do with itThe fix
Password
Critical		 Log in as you there, and on every other site where you used the same password. Criminals test leaked passwords against banks, email and shops automatically. Change it on that site. Then change it everywhere you reused it, starting with your email account. Turn on two-step sign-in or a passkey. Sign out of other sessions. 10 min
Bank or card details
Critical		 Make purchases, or call you pretending to be your bank, quoting real digits to sound genuine. Ask your bank to replace the card, using the number on the back of it. Check recent transactions. Turn on payment alerts in your banking app. 10 min
ID numbers
passport, driving licence, National Insurance, Social Security
Critical		 Open accounts or apply for credit in your name. Pass identity checks. US: freeze your credit at Equifax, Experian and TransUnion. It is free and you can lift it when you need credit. UK: check your credit file with the three agencies (free) and look for searches you don’t recognise. If the passport or licence itself was lost, report it. 20 min
Date of birth
High		 Answer security questions, and make a scam call far more convincing. Combined with your address it is most of an identity. You can’t change it, so make it less useful: add two-step sign-in to email and banking, and treat any caller who “confirms” your birthday as unverified. 5 min
Security questions and answers
High		 Reset your password on any site that uses the same questions. Change the answers there and anywhere else you used them. Better: use a made-up answer stored in a password manager. 5 min
Home address
High		 Post you convincing letters, add detail to scams, and add you to people-search listings. Nothing to rotate. Lower its reach instead: see where it is already public (our free home-address guide covers this) and watch for post you didn’t ask for. —

Step 2 · Decode it, continued

What leakedWhat someone can do with itThe fix
Phone number
Medium		 Scam calls and texts that name the breached company. In the worst case, a “SIM swap”: persuading your network to move your number to their SIM, then receiving your security codes. Ask your mobile network to add a PIN or password to your account for any number transfer or SIM change. Move two-step sign-in for email and banking from text messages to an app or passkey. 10 min
Email address only
Medium		 Phishing that mentions the breached company by name, so it looks legitimate. Expect emails “from” that company for a few months. Go to its site yourself rather than clicking links. Consider a separate address, or an alias, for new sign-ups. 1 min
Name, username, gender, job title
Low		 Little on its own. Useful to tailor a scam once combined with the rest. No action on its own. Fix the higher rows first. —
Health, sexuality, religion, children’s data
Critical		 Embarrassment, discrimination or extortion. Rare, but the most personal harm. Be very wary of anyone who contacts you referring to it. Don’t pay or reply; report it to the police. In the UK or EU, you can complain to the data protection regulator about how the company protected it. —
Logins taken from your own device
“stealer log”, “infostealer”, “malware”
Critical · do first		 This is different: the leak was your computer or phone, not a company. Everything saved in that browser may be known, including sessions that skip two-step sign-in. 1. Scan and clean the device, or reset it. 2. From a different, clean device, change your email password first, then banking, then your password manager, then the rest. 3. Sign out of all sessions everywhere. 4. In your email settings, delete any forwarding rules or recovery addresses you didn’t add. 45–60 min

Why the order matters

Your email account is the master key: almost every other password can be reset through it. If you only have ten minutes, secure your email first, then your bank, then the breached site.

Step 3 · Do it

Your next 30 minutes, most dangerous first.

A small study of real breach victims found only about a third changed the leaked password at all, and many chose one just as weak. Ticking these off already puts you ahead.

Secure your email account

New, unique password. Two-step sign-in with an app or passkey. Check forwarding rules and recovery details.

8 min

Fix every critical row from pages 3–4

Password reuse, card details, ID numbers, device logins. One at a time; tick each.

10–20 min

Add a PIN to your mobile account

Stops someone moving your number to their SIM.

5 min

Ask one question about the breached company

Do I still use it? If not, delete the account (look for “delete account” in settings), or ask the company to erase your data. Data that no longer exists can’t leak in the next breach.
